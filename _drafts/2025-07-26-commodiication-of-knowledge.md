As knowledge-seeking has become resoundingly abridged, quantified, and commodified, 
the world has become populated by these plastic palaces of data. To stay relevant here, 
the humanities are forced to venture into the faceless *Digital Humanities*, which generally
 consists of compressing archival and/or literary works into large quantities of data and 
 finding general patterns about them. (For example, the Google n-gram viewer). 
 Though there is merit in this, each of these works contain a world unto itself. 
 And that is what is lost in the data-fication [sic?] of human experience.

Given my disposition,
 I am easily bored by charts, percentages, averages, medians, and standard deviations, in such fields
  that involve experience. (Okay… here, note that statistics was the discipline in math
   that was the most bewildering to me — so you can take everything I say about it with
    a grain of salt in my presumably salty take.) I am taunted by those little disembodied voices
     calmly demanding: *Where is the evidence? Show me stats!* But when you tell me 73% of car-drivers 
     believe in so-and-so, I am much more interested in that 27% that could not be categorized in the majority. 
     *Why* do they believe in this way and not that? This is my main qualm with any type of psychology research
      (being once subjected to countless of those experimental surveys as a university student): 
      it tends to generalize the behavior, intentions, beliefs of the average to *everyone.* And then we get deluged with 
      Psychology Today articles, “Science says…” headlines, and it even enters the vernacular with bloggers citing percentages 
      from research studies. Then, people think these averaged findings apply to everyone. See the [problem of non-ergodicity]. 

To me, data storytelling is a sorrowful venture. Stories resist being told en masse. 
Stories are about idiosyncrasies, individualized tales and narratives that you have inherited from your life experiences,
 your family’s, your culture’s. Stories are the vehicle of un-selfing, so the expression goes. … 
 Perhaps it would be better to call it “data myth-making" or ‘data legend-weaving,” 
 since data is trying to paint a generalized topography/cosmogony of the world or a certain group of people. 

Data storytelling, and its ilk, do not come out of a vacuum, however 
(that would be bad storytelling), it is the fruit of a long history of knowledge-seeking.
 I read this line from a very intriguing old [blog post](https://neilgreenberg.com/ao-knowledge-is-power/) 
 (lecture notes?) by a researcher named Neil Greenberg. In it, he untangles the meaning of “Knowledge is power.”  
 He discusses Einstein’s commitment to uncover the fundamentals over the details that “clutter the mind.” Greenberg 
 ties this in with Thomas Merton’s quote: 
 
 >  “To allow oneself to be carried away by a multitude of conflicting concerns, to surrender to too many demands, to commit to too many projects, to want to help everyone in everything is itself to succumb to the violence of our times. Frenzy destroys our inner capacity for peace.  It destroys the fruitfulness of our work, because it kills the root of inner wisdom which makes work fruitful.”

Greenberg states that, 
> It is reasonable to expect anything of power to be valued and commodified. 
 Knowledge management is a field of study and one focus is often the [[DIKW hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIKW_pyramid)]…
  As information moves from data to information to knowledge to wisdom, value is added at each level.
