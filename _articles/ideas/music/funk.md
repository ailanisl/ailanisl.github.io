The funk!

Whereas Motown provided the musical voice for the Black middle-class aspirations, the funk movement came as a reactionary musical alternative. It uprooted the traditional African-American downbeat structure by emphasizing “The One,” the downbeat in a four-beat bar. “Landing on The One,” says scholar and musician Jason King, “does something magical, it literally mobilizes you and makes you want to shake your–er–something!” (Nelson, 2025) 

Funk engendered a raucous break from the prim and proper—from “the intense commodification and mass consumption of an organic black music form”—and instead offered a reconnection with African polyrhythms. According to author Rickey Vincent, the New Orleans music tradition was particularly important in the genesis of funk, in that 
> The polyrhythmic emphasis of New Orleans drumming was a specialty of the region—the only region in the United States where black slaves were allowed to play drums from day one….The very African quality of performing with multiple rhythms playing simultaneously—in a lively  syncopation—was retained by the people in the region and has spread through New Orleans Dixieland Jazz, ragtime, rhythm and blues, and, ultimately, The Funk. (1996)
