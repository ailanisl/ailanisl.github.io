I read this book the summer before heading to college. At the time, I was obsessed about learning Indian cultures -- from my journal: "Today, I read Mishra's book, listened to Bollywood and Sufi music, ate Indian curries for dinner and finished watching *Highway*." (It was my go-to comfort film. A Bollywood film about a rich city girl (played by Alia Bhatt) getting kidnapped and developing Stockholm syndrome for the open world of the Himalayas.. and her captor.) Though painfully simple and a questionable premise, I could watch this over and over again. I think it says something about what I desired at this point in my life: I wrote in my notes, "The beauty of the Himalayas and the pastoral/shepherdic life is intoxicating. I'm drunk with the subtle desire to sit in those history- and spirituality-riddled alcoves, to be devoured by solitude and quiet. To drown in the comforting heartbeat of nature." 

I was intoxicated with this image I fashioned in my mind. Yes, this was my high Romantic phase, as evidenced by the dramatic prose. I feel like many people (especially the disillusioned children of the middle class) has a period of life like this. And Mishra's book is also a testament. 

Though he's from India, Mishra had a very Western education, to the point where he disdained the older schools of thought coming from his own country. It was only later in his life when he explored the native myths and spiritualities of his sub-continent that he garnered an reverence for them. Yet, the irony is that the Buddhism he talks of in this book is very much painted in Western colors. I didn't have a word for it at the time, but this book is an example of Buddhist modernism. A form of Buddhism that tries to meld it with Romanticism, Enlightenment Rationality, and in some respects, Protestantism (in an effort to acculturate it to Western soils, prove its legitimacy to Western minds). This Buddhism claims it is therapeutic (mindfulness-based), backed by empiricism and compatible with modern science, relies on the individual rather than institutions or external framewords, the inward focus on the self and subjectivity -- all of which are the hallmarks of Western modernity.
