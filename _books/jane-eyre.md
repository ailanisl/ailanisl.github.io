It was enjoyable to read. I imagine mid-19th century folks engaged with such novels as we do with Netflix (but perhaps not as democratized). 

And I appreciate that the main characters are plain or common in appearance. Reminds me of British shows that value acting talent over looks. 

The first-person narrative was jarring at first – I was not really expecting the intimacy in a novel this old. I don't know what I was expecting going into the book, but I vividly remember a childhood friend saying his mother (a high school English teacher) really loved the book. My mom also read this when she was in grade school. 

Impressions: incredibly influenced by Romanticism, mentions of Schiller (Jane studied German with the Rivers sisters) and allusions to Blake and Shelley. 

The prose was blunt, I was rather unimpressed with Charlotte's writing style. I believe the pacing (though a bit awkward in places) is good, as well as the place-setting. The only passages that I've been struck by are her/Jane's loving descriptions of nature, the moon, the sky, and all. 

What suprised me most was all the talk of phrenology and, accordingly, a fixation on physical description. It says in the notes (of my Penguin edition) that Charlotte's father had a popular book on phrenology, and there is no doubt that the pseudoscience seeped into her writing. Though, of course,  we would call a lot of things from back then a 'pseudoscience,' as many things today may well be labeled such in the future. The apppeal of phrenology is, I think, much like the appeal of palm-reading. Humans will try to make sense of the chaos we are thrown into at birth with whatever materials we are given in this ephemeral life – be it our bodies, our psychologies, our tools and technology, or what have you. 

Mr. Rochester to Jane:
> Know that... you will often find yourself elected the involuntary confidante of your acquantances' secrets: people will instinctively find out...that it is not your forte to tell of yourself, but to listen while others talk of themselves; they will fell, too, that you listen with no malevolent scorn of thier indiscretion, but with a kind of innate sympathy; not the less comforting and encouraging because it is very unobtrusive in its manifestations. 

He may be talking to me.. People usually want someone to listen to them, not advice. 

Jane describing her experience of St John's sermon: 
> I experienced an inexpressible sadness...the eloquence to which I had been listening had sprang from a depth where lay turbid dregs of diappointment and disquieting aspirations. I was sure St. John Rivers - pure-lived, conscientious, zealous as he was– had not yet found that peace of God which passeth all understanding. (p. 405)

A theme of restlessness of mind recurs. 

The strong Christian morality and the missionary zeal of St. John was off-putting and sour-tasting (from my modern perch). Unfortunate that the last passages expounded on this.
